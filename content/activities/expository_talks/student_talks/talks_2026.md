## EP2609: Chebotarev Density Theorem

- **Speaker:** Gauransh Kapur (B. Math, 2027)
- **Abstract:** Dirichlet's Theorem on Primes in Arithmetic Progression states that there are infinitely many primes of the form \\(a+nb\\) for coprime \\(a,b\\) and gives their density as \\(1/\phi(b)\\) where \\(\phi\\) is the Euler totient function. Chebotarev's theorem is one of the many generalisations of this result. It is a foundational result in class field theory and has many applications to the theory of Elliptic Curves and Galois Representations. We recap some Galois theory and algebraic number theory, define the Artin symbol, and talk about ramification in relative extensions. We also prove some applications of this theorem, such as Dirichlet's Theorem and uniqueness of Galois extensions with the same set of totally split primes (up to finite difference, will be made precise).
If time permits, we talk about the decomposition of integer polynomials modulo primes and sketch the proof that a primitive positive definite quadratic form represents infinitely many primes.
- **Video:** [TBU]
- **Date and TIme:** Sunday, 11th October 2026, 11 am - 12:30 pm
- **Venue:** 2nd Floor Auditorium, Academic Building

## EP2608: Convex Bodies and Brunn-Minkowski Theory

- **Speaker:** Ramdas Singh (B. Math, 2027)
- **Abstract:** Convex bodies are concrete geometric objects, but their collection \\(\mathcal{K}^n\\) carries a rich algebraic structure under Minkowski addition. This talk explores the linear and metric properties of \\(\mathcal{K}^n\\), specifically its isometric embedding into the Banach space of continuous functions on the unit sphere. We then pivot to the irreducibly non-linear behavior of volume to prove the Brunn-Minkowski inequality. Finally, we will leverage this concavity to establish the isoperimetric inequality, confirming the Euclidean ball has the smallest surface area for a given volume. Some elementary results from functional analysis, topology, and measure theory will be used, but all necessary prerequisites will be stated without proof.
- **Video:** [TBU]
- **Date and TIme:** Saturday, 10th October 2026, 11 am - 12:30 pm
- **Venue:** G-26 Classroom, Academic Building

## EP2607: Curve Shortening Flow: Curvature, Diffusion and the Evolution of Plane Curves

- **Speaker:** Kanak Nilesh Sanghvi (B. Math, 2027)
- **Abstract:** Curve Shortening Flow is a geometric evolution in which a plane curve evolves in the normal direction with speed proportional to its curvature. We will develop the basic differential geometry of plane curves and derive the evolution equations for arclength, length, and curvature. We will then interpret the resulting nonlinear parabolic PDE and use the maximum principle to understand preservation of convexity and the smoothing behaviour of the flow. The talk will conclude with an overview of how these ideas lead to the eventual rounding and extinction of embedded closed curves.
- **Video:** [TBU]
- **Date and Time:** Sunday, 28th September 2026, 5:30 PM - 6:30 PM
- **Venue:** 2nd Floor Auditorium, Academic Building

## EP2606: Can eigenvalues determine a graph? A First  Journey into Spectral Graph Theory

- **Speaker:** Debatra Das (M. Math, 2027, ISI Kolkata)
- **Abstract:** Graphs may look like simple collections of points and lines, but their eigenvalues often reveal much deeper structure. In this talk, I will take the audience on a short journey into spectral graph theory, centered around a beautiful question: can a graph be uniquely determined by the spectrum of its adjacency or Laplacian matrix?  
  &nbsp;&nbsp;We will see examples of graphs that are determined by their spectra, as well as surprising cases where different graphs share exactly the same eigenvalues. Along the way, I will discuss why this question is important and what it tells us about the relationship between algebra and graph structure. I will end with a brief overview of Matthew Kwan’s remarkable result, which shows how modern combinatorial ideas can be used to study spectral uniqueness.  
  &nbsp;&nbsp;The goal of the talk is not to assume much background, but to share some of the excitement of a subject where algebra, combinatorics, and geometry come together through the language of eigenvalues.
- **Video:** Available [here](https://youtu.be/fyO0nQHxymM)
- **Date and Time:** Sunday, 16th August 2026, 11:00 AM - 1:00 PM
- **Venue:** Online

## EP2605: Counting Arcs on Punctured Discs

- **Speaker:** Pragalbh Kumar Awasthi (M. Math, 2027)
- **Abstract:** In this talk, we will discuss a sharp extremal problem concerning collections of essential arcs on punctured disks. Specifically, we consider families of pairwise non-homotopic arcs with endpoints on the boundary of a punctured disk, under the condition that any two arcs intersect at most twice. We will present the proof of the optimal upper bound on the size of such a family, together with constructions showing that the bound is sharp. Along the way, we will see how local intersection patterns lead to global constraints, illustrating the rich interplay between topology and combinatorics in the study of surfaces.
- **Video:** Available [here](https://youtu.be/X4N398B2ZHk)
- **Date and Time:** Sunday, 9th August 2026, 11:00 AM - 1:00 PM
- **Venue:** 2nd Floor Auditorium, Academic Building

## EP2604: A Happy Ending

- **Speaker:** Hrishik Koley (M.Sc. CS, 2028, CMI)
- **Abstract:** How many points must we place in the plane before some \\(k\\) of them are forced to form a convex polygon? Starting from the elementary fact that five points in general position always contain a convex quadrilateral, we introduce the Erdős-Szekeres function \\(ES(k)\\) and the celebrated Happy Ending conjecture. We then explore a connection with the Erdős-Szekeres monotone subsequence theorem, more precisely it's geometric analogue, as well as a special case of Ramsey's theorem called the Ramsey's theorem for triples.
- **Video:** Available [here](https://youtu.be/LYTpZJ0Mte8)
- **Date and Time:** Tuesday, 28th July 2026, 6:00 - 7:00 PM
- **Venue:** 2nd Floor Auditorium, Academic Building

## EP2603: Cutting a Cake in \\(n^{n^{n^{n^{n^n}}}}\\) Steps

- **Speaker:** Shankha Suvra Dam (B. Math, 2026)
- **Abstract:** How hard can it be to cut a cake fairly? If you've ever had to split a cake with various toppings, you know the struggle: everyone wants a different piece! How can you ensure the distribution is fair? And mathematically speaking, how do you even go about defining what "fair" looks like in this context?  
  &nbsp;&nbsp;That is exactly what we will discuss in this talk. We will start by defining the rigorous notions surrounding fairness and then review the foundational body of work in this field. All of this will culminate in the monumental 2016 result by H. Aziz and S. Mackenzie, which finally provided an upper bound (admittedly, a completely ridiculous one) for the number of steps required to guarantee a fair division of a cake among \\(n\\) agents.

## EP2601: Introduction to Weak Derivatives and Sobolev Spaces

- **Speaker:** Ramdas Singh (B. Math, 2027)
- **Abstract:** Classical differentiation is often too rigid: functions that behave well under integration may fail to have pointwise derivatives. This talk introduces weak differentiation, motivated by integration by parts and the Lebesgue integral. We define weak derivatives, examine basic examples, and show how classical results extend in this setting, leading naturally to the Sobolev space \\(H^1\\). The Poincaré inequality is also discussed as a first illustration of why such spaces are central in analysis and variational problems. A working knowledge of Lebesgue integration and normed and inner product spaces may be required.
- **Video:** Available [here](https://youtu.be/5SpskFIcBhI)
- **Date and Time:** Sunday, 18th January 2026, 11:00 - 1:00 PM
- **Venue:** G-26 Classroom, Academic Building
